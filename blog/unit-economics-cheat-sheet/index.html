Unit economics in 12 minutes: the founder's cheat sheet

By Joshua Wark · ~12 min read · MBA Rock

Unit economics is the single most-asked-about topic on every founder podcast, in every accelerator deck review, and at every investor pitch — and it's almost never explained in a way you can use on Monday morning.

Here's the no-fluff version. Twelve minutes. Four metrics, three ratios, what good looks like, and the three traps that catch every first-time founder.

What unit economics actually means

Unit economics is the math of one unit of your business. One customer. One subscription. One transaction. If the math of one unit works, the business can scale. If the math of one unit doesn't work, scaling makes everything worse — you're losing money on every new customer, just faster.

If your unit economics are bad, scale is your enemy, not your friend.

Every other business question — pricing, marketing spend, hiring, fundraising — sits downstream of this question: does one customer pay back more than it costs you to serve them?

The four numbers you need

1. Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

The fully-loaded cost to win one new customer. Marketing spend, sales spend, ad spend, content costs — divided by the number of new customers acquired in the same window.

CAC = total acquisition spend ÷ new customers in the period

Pitfall: most founders only count ad spend. CAC has to include your time, the contractor who wrote the landing page, the email tools, the salary slice of whoever runs your sales. If it isn't fully-loaded, you'll over-invest in the cheapest-looking channel and miss that it actually has the worst CAC.

2. Lifetime Value (LTV)

The total revenue a customer generates over the entire relationship — not their first purchase. Subscription businesses think about this naturally. Transactional businesses often miss it.

LTV = average order value × orders per year × years of retention

For a subscription business: monthly revenue per customer × expected months retained. For an e-commerce business: average order value × repeat purchase rate × expected lifetime in years.

The trap here is using revenue instead of gross margin. We'll fix that in the next number.

3. Contribution Margin

The piece of each dollar of revenue that's left over after the variable costs of serving that customer. Not after rent. Not after salaries. Just the costs that scale with each unit — payment processing, hosting, raw materials, fulfillment.

Contribution margin = revenue per unit − variable cost per unit

If you sell a $50 SKU and it costs $18 to ship it (cost of goods + packaging + payment processing + last-mile), your contribution margin per unit is $32, or 64%. This is the number that has to cover everything else in your business.

4. Payback Period

How many months it takes for one customer to pay back their own CAC. The single most underrated unit-economics metric.

Payback (months) = CAC ÷ (monthly revenue × contribution margin)

A short payback period means cash returns fast — you can recycle it into more acquisition. A long payback period means you need outside capital to grow, because your cash gets tied up before it returns to you.

The three ratios that decide whether you have a business

Ratio 1 — LTV : CAC

The headline ratio. How much value does a customer return relative to the cost of winning them?

Ratio
Verdict
What to do
< 1:1
Losing money per customer
Stop spending; fix the model.
1:1 – 2:1
Marginal
Profitability requires near-zero overhead.
3:1
The textbook target
Healthy, room to invest in growth.
5:1 +
Underspending on acquisition
You're leaving growth on the table.

The 3:1 number is folklore-grade but useful as a center of gravity. Below 3:1, you're probably under-pricing or over-spending. Above 5:1, you're often under-investing in growth and a competitor with a better-funded acquisition engine will eventually overtake you.

Ratio 2 — Payback Period

How fast your cash comes back. SaaS and subscription operators target under 12 months. Direct-to-consumer e-commerce typically targets under 6. Enterprise SaaS can stretch to 18–24 if the LTV justifies it.

If your payback is longer than your runway, you're not unit-economic-positive — you're capital-dependent. That's not a death sentence, but it's a different game (and a different pitch).

Ratio 3 — Contribution Margin %

What's left from each dollar of revenue after the cost to serve. The benchmark to clear depends on your category — software typically over 70%, branded CPG often 50–60%, marketplaces 15–35%. The number you're looking at less than the number's trajectory: is it climbing, flat, or eroding as you scale?

The three traps that catch every first-time founder

Trap 1 — Mistaking revenue for LTV

If you use top-line revenue instead of margin-adjusted revenue, your LTV is fictional. A 30% contribution margin business with "$10K LTV" actually has $3K of usable LTV. That changes every downstream decision.

Trap 2 — Cohort drift

Your earliest customers retain better than your later ones almost every time, because they're product-fit early adopters. If you average all cohorts, you'll over-estimate LTV. Always look at cohort-specific retention curves.

Trap 3 — CAC blended with referral

If 40% of your customers come from word-of-mouth (with $0 CAC) and the other 60% come from paid ads (with $250 CAC), your blended CAC of $150 hides the truth. Paid CAC is going up. Referral CAC is structurally limited. You're shipping a number that makes both invisible.

Always separate paid CAC from blended CAC. The paid number is what's actually moveable.

What to do tomorrow morning

Pick the one number above you couldn't have recited before reading this. Find it for your business. Write it on a sticky note. Stick it where you'll see it every Monday.

If it's payback period, give yourself 30 days to compress it by one month. If it's contribution margin, look at every variable line item and ask whether any of them can drop two percentage points without breaking the product. If it's LTV, instrument cohort retention.

Founders who lose this game aren't usually the ones with bad ideas. They're the ones who never got fluent in the four numbers above and built a business on a model that couldn't actually return the cash that funded it.
