Cash flow is oxygen: the first framework every founder needs

By Joshua Wark · ~4 min read · MBA Rock

Most founders learn three financial metrics in this order:

  1. Revenue
  2. Valuation
  3. Cash flow

That ordering kills businesses. The right ordering is:

  1. Cash flow
  2. Revenue
  3. Valuation

And it's not even close.

Why cash flow is oxygen

You can be unprofitable for years. You can be unsexy. You can have terrible margins, slow growth, no story for an investor. You can survive all of that.

You cannot survive running out of cash.

Cash is oxygen. The moment you stop breathing, you stop. There is no comeback story for a company that missed payroll three Fridays in a row. The narrative ends when the bank balance hits zero.

Revenue is vanity. Profit is sanity. Cash is oxygen.

This is the single most underrated reframe in early-stage business. Most founders intellectually know it. Almost none operate by it. Their dashboard tracks revenue. Their decisions optimize for revenue. Their pitch focuses on revenue. The bank balance is checked when it's already late.

The three numbers

If I had to pick the three numbers every founder should know cold, every day, they're:

  • Cash position today. Your bank balance plus near-term collectibles.
  • Monthly net burn. Cash out minus cash in, monthly.
  • Runway in months. Cash position divided by burn.

That's it. Three numbers. If you can't recite all three from memory right now, you're flying blind on the only metric that decides if you survive.

The math

The math is embarrassingly simple. If you have $80K in the bank and you're burning $20K a month, you have four months of oxygen. That's not eight months. That's not "I'll figure something out." That's four months until you make payroll out of personal savings or lose the business.

If your monthly burn shrinks to $10K, you have eight months. If your monthly burn grows to $40K, you have two.

Every founder I've ever worked with who's blown up did the same thing: they let one of those numbers get away from them. Usually it was burn. Sometimes it was a single big customer collecting late. The cause varies. The effect is always the same.
